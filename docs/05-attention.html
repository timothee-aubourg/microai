Chapter 5

Letting tokens look at each other

After chapter 4, each token's vector knows about that token and nothing else. The next character depends on everything before it. How does information travel from where it is to where it is needed?

The count table of chapter 1 had a fixed answer: look at the last few characters, always the same ones. That is rigid. To continue KING RICHARD: the model needs the line break that follows a speaker's name. To finish a word it needs the letters since the last space. What is relevant changes from position to position.

So let each position choose what to read, based on content.

Definition

Attention is the step in which every token collects information from the tokens before it, taking more from the ones that matter to it and less from the others.

It is built from the dot product of chapter 4, plus two new tools. Take the tools first.

Tool one: a matrix turns a vector into another vector

Definition

A matrix is a table of numbers. Multiplying a vector by a matrix gives a new vector: each entry of the new vector is the dot product of the old vector with one column of the table.

By hand

Take the vector (2, 1) and a matrix whose two columns are (1, 0) and (0.5, −1).

Entry of the new vectorDot product of (2, 1) withResult
firstcolumn 1: (1, 0)2 × 1 + 1 × 0 = 2
secondcolumn 2: (0.5, −1)2 × 0.5 + 1 × −1 = 0

The vector (2, 1) has become (2, 0). A matrix is a way to compute a new vector from an old one, and what it computes depends on the numbers in the table. In a model those numbers are parameters, so training decides what each matrix does. Most of the 345,504 parameters of our model sit in matrices.

Tool two: the softmax turns scores into shares

The second tool takes a list of scores, any numbers at all, and turns it into shares: positive numbers that add up to 1, larger for larger scores.

Definition

The softmax of a list of scores: raise e ≈ 2.718 to the power of each score, then divide each result by their total.

sharei = escoreiescore1 + escore2 + …

By hand

Three scores: 2, 0 and 1.

The shares add up to 1, and the highest score has the largest share. Each part of the recipe answers a need:

  • Positive, and adding up to 1. A power of e is never negative, and dividing by the total makes the sum 1. So the shares can be used as the weights of an average, or as probabilities.
  • Smooth. A small change in a score gives a small change in the shares. Gradient descent needs that: it follows slopes, and a rule like "give everything to the largest" has none.
  • Only differences count. Add 10 to every score and the shares do not change. A gap of 1 between two scores always means that one share is e ≈ 2.7 times the other, as with 0.24 and 0.09 above.

The softmax is used in two places. Inside attention, as you are about to see. And at the very end of the model, where it turns the 65 scores of chapter 4 into the 65 probabilities that chapter 1 asked for.

Ask, advertise, hand over

Now attention itself. From its vector, every token produces three new vectors, each by multiplying with its own learned matrix:

Definitions

A token's query says what it is looking for.

Its key says what it can offer.

Its value is what it hands over if it is chosen.

A token compares its query with the key of every token before it, using the dot product. A high score means "this is what I was looking for". The softmax turns the scores into shares. The token then receives the average of the values, each weighted by its share. That average is added to the token's own vector, and the token now knows something about its context.

By hand: one token reads three others

A token has the query (1, 0). Three tokens stand before it:

Earlier tokenKeyScore: query · keyShare (softmax)Value
first(2, 0)1 × 2 + 0 × 0 = 20.67(1, 0)
second(0, 1)1 × 0 + 0 × 1 = 00.09(0, 1)
third(1, 1)1 × 1 + 0 × 1 = 10.24(1, 1)

The scores are 2, 0 and 1, the same three as in the softmax example, so the shares are 0.67, 0.09 and 0.24. What the token receives is the weighted average of the values:

0.67 × (1, 0) + 0.09 × (0, 1) + 0.24 × (1, 1) = (0.91, 0.33)

Two thirds of what it received came from the first token, whose key pointed the same way as its query. Nothing was looked up by position. The first token was chosen for what it contained.

Attention with five tokens, in two dimensions

Try this. The dark arrow is one token's query. The coloured dots are the keys of five other tokens, and each token's value is simply its colour. Read the bars as they stand. Swing the query toward another key and watch the output take that colour. Then pull sharpness to 0, and to 5.

What you should have seen. At the start the query points near the key of sun: its score is 4.0 and its share 87.1%, with 6.7% for rose and 6.0% for ash, so the output is close to the colour of sun. At sharpness 0 every score is 0 and every share is 20.0%: the output is the plain average of all five colours, and the token reads everything and learns little. At sharpness 5 sun has 100.0%: the output is a copy of one value. Attention can sit anywhere between a soft blend and a precise lookup, and the model learns which it needs.

Why divide by a square root?

The attention formula in every implementation divides the scores by √d, where d is the number of dimensions of the queries and keys. It looks like a fussy detail. It is there because of a fact about dot products that you can check by experiment.

A dot product is a sum of d terms. Suppose the entries of the two vectors are unrelated numbers of a typical size of 1. Each term is then as likely to be positive as negative. Such a sum does not stay near zero, and it does not grow as fast as d either, because the terms partly cancel: its typical size grows as √d. With 2 dimensions the scores are around ±1.4. With 1,024 they are around ±32. Feed scores that far apart to a softmax and one share becomes 1 and the rest 0.

Random queries and keys, at different widths

Try this. Eight random keys, one random query. Move the slider from 2 dimensions to 8, 64 and 1,024, and compare the two sets of bars and the line under them.

What you should have seen. The typical size of a score follows √d: 1.3 at 2 dimensions, 2.8 at 8, 7.7 at 64 and 31.3 at 1,024. Without the division, the largest of the eight shares grows from 41% to 64%, 88% and 97%: attention collapses onto one token as the vectors get wider. With the division it stays near 35% at every width.

A collapsed softmax is a problem for learning, and not only for reading. Where a share is pinned at 0 or 1 the slope is nearly flat, so the gradient has nothing to say, and at the start of training the model would be stuck with hard choices it made by accident. Dividing by √d brings the scores back to a typical size of 1 whatever the width, and leaves it to training to sharpen them where sharpness helps.

No looking ahead

One rule is missing. During training the model sees a whole passage and must predict, at every position, the character that follows. If position 5 could read position 6, it would just read the answer.

Definition

The causal mask stops every position from reading the positions after it: before the softmax, their scores are replaced by minus infinity, which gives them a share of exactly 0.

By hand: take the three scores 2, 0 and 1 again, and suppose the third token comes after the reading token. Its score is replaced by minus infinity, and e to that power is 0. The softmax is then taken over 7.39, 1.00 and 0: the shares are 0.88, 0.12 and 0. The later token contributes nothing.

The mask is what lets one pass over a passage of 64 characters count as 64 honest predictions, each made from the characters before it and nothing else.

Several heads

One pattern of shares per position is limiting: a token may want its previous letter and the start of the line.

Definition

A head is one complete copy of the attention step, with its own queries, keys and values. Several heads run side by side on the same tokens, and each can look for something different.

In our model the 96 numbers of each vector are split into 4 groups of 24, and each group is one head. Their outputs are put back side by side into 96 numbers and mixed by one more matrix. The model also applies the whole attention step three times in a row, in three layers. Chapter 6 explains the layers; here you only need to know that the widget below lets you pick one.

Attention inside the trained model

Try this. The last character, the r of our, is selected. The shading shows how much it reads from each earlier position, in the chosen layer and head. Step through the four heads of layer 1. Then choose layer 2, head 2. Tap other characters as you go.

What you should have seen. In layer 1, head 2 reads the character just before: the r takes 67% from the u one place back and 17% from the o two back. The other heads of layer 1 also read the last few characters, in different proportions. Layer 2, head 2 does something else: the same r takes 50% from the line break 24 places back, the one after KING RICHARD:.

This holds beyond one example. Measured over 2,500 characters of held-out text, the heads of layer 1 all do a version of the same job: they read the last few characters. Head 2 is the sharpest, with about half its weight on the character immediately before. The model has rebuilt for itself the local context that the count table of chapter 1 was given by construction. Layer 2, head 2 does something a table cannot: it sends about half its attention to the most recent line break, however far back that is. In a play script that is worth knowing, because what comes next depends on whether a line has just begun.

Build it

def attention(self, x, i, trace=None):
    B, T, d = x.shape
    h, hd = self.n_head, d // self.n_head
    split = lambda t: t.reshape(B, T, h, hd).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3)   # (B, heads, T, hd)
    q = split(x @ self.p[f'h{i}.attn.wq'])
    k = split(x @ self.p[f'h{i}.attn.wk'])
    v = split(x @ self.p[f'h{i}.attn.wv'])
    scores = (q @ k.transpose(0, 1, 3, 2)) * (1.0 / np.sqrt(hd))     # every query · every key
    future = np.triu(np.ones((T, T), dtype=bool), k=1)               # no peeking ahead
    weights = scores.masked_fill(future, -1e9).softmax(axis=-1)      # each row sums to 1
    if trace is not None:
        trace.setdefault('attn', []).append(weights.data)
    mixed = weights @ v                                              # weighted average of values
    mixed = mixed.transpose(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(B, T, d)             # glue the heads back
    return mixed @ self.p[f'h{i}.attn.wo']

From code/gpt.py. Queries, keys, values, scores, the scale, the mask, the softmax and the weighted average, for all positions and all heads at once. x holds the vectors of every position, @ is multiplication by a matrix, and wq, wk, wv are the three matrices that make queries, keys and values. wo is the matrix that mixes the heads at the end.

What this chapter glossed over

The √d argument has an assumption. It holds when the entries of queries and keys are roughly independent with similar spread. That is true at the start of training, and a step described in chapter 6 helps keep it approximately true later. It is a statement about typical size, not a guarantee.

An attention map is not an explanation. It shows where a head reads from. It does not show what was read or how it was used afterwards. Treat pretty patterns as hints.

The cost grows with the square of the length. Every position scores every earlier position, so doubling the context quadruples the work in this step. This is the main reason long contexts are expensive, and chapter 8 shows the trick that makes generation affordable anyway.

In short

  • Attention lets each token collect information from the tokens before it, chosen by content. Each token makes a query, a key and a value by multiplying its vector with three learned matrices.
  • Query · key gives a score for every earlier token. The softmax turns scores into shares that add up to 1. The token receives the values, averaged with those shares. By hand, scores of 2, 0 and 1 gave shares of 0.67, 0.09 and 0.24.
  • Scores are divided by √d, or wide vectors would make the softmax pick one token and ignore the rest: 97% on one token at 1,024 dimensions without it, about 35% with it.
  • The causal mask gives later positions a share of 0, so every position predicts from what came before.
  • Several heads run side by side. In our model one reads the previous character, and another finds the last line break.

Check yourself

The scores are 3 and 1. Without a calculator: which share is larger, and by what factor?

The first, by a factor of e × e ≈ 7.4. A gap of 1 between scores is a factor of e between shares, and the gap here is 2. The shares are 0.88 and 0.12.

In the first widget, what is the output when sharpness is 0?

All scores become 0, the softmax gives every token a share of 1/5, and the output is the plain average of the five values, whatever the query is.

Our heads use 24 dimensions. What are the scores multiplied by?

1/√24 ≈ 0.204. You can see it in the code as 1.0 / np.sqrt(hd).

What would happen if you trained without the mask and then asked the model to generate?

Training loss would drop to almost zero, because every position could copy its answer from the next one. At generation time there is no next one. The model would have learned to cheat and nothing else.
