Chapter 1

Guess the next character

How can a file full of numbers write a sentence?

Every AI system that writes text, from the small model on this course's home page to the assistant on your phone, is built around one surprisingly modest piece. This chapter is about that piece: what it does, how to build the simplest possible version of it, how to measure whether it is any good, and why the simplest version hits a wall. Each of these ideas comes back in every later chapter, so we take them slowly.

The one thing a language model does

Before any definitions, a picture. Below, a model has been given the text ROMEO: Wh. It does not answer with a letter. It answers with a list of scores, one for every character that could come next, saying how likely each one is.

That list of scores is what the whole field is built on, so it deserves precise words.

Definitions

A probability is a number between 0 and 1 that says how likely something is: 0 means never, 1 means always, 0.25 means one time in four.

A probability distribution is a list of probabilities, one for each possible outcome, that adds up to exactly 1. Something has to happen, so the chances of all the outcomes together are 100%.

A language model is a program that takes some text and returns a probability distribution over the next symbol: a probability for every symbol that could come next. In this chapter a symbol is a single character: a letter, a digit, a space or a punctuation mark.

Refresher: probabilities in two minutes

A fair die gives each face probability 1/6 ≈ 0.167. The six probabilities add up to 1. A loaded die might give the six a probability of 0.5 and each other face 0.1: still a distribution, because 0.5 + 5 × 0.1 = 1.

"Rolling" a loaded die means picking one outcome so that, over many rolls, each outcome turns up as often as its probability says. Picking at random in this weighted way is called sampling.

The probability of two independent things both happening is the product of their probabilities: two sixes in a row on a fair die is 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36. Keep this in mind: it is why logarithms appear later in this chapter.

That is all a language model does. It does not write, answer or chat. Sampling once from the distribution gives one character. Writing a sentence is a loop: ask for the distribution, sample a character, append it to the text, ask again. Chatting (chapter 10) and agents using tools (chapter 11) are the same loop with extra rules around it.

The whole difficulty therefore sits in one place: how do you compute good probabilities? Start with the simplest answer anyone could give.

Just count

Suppose that all the text we have is one short sentence, and we want to predict what comes after the letter t. The simplest thing to do is look at every t in the sentence and note what comes right after it:

Four times, something followed a t: an h twice and a space twice. Turn those tallies into fractions by dividing by the total:

After t cameTallyProbability
h22 / 4 = 0.5
␣ (space)22 / 4 = 0.5
anything else00

The probabilities add up to 1, so this is a distribution: a language model, made of nothing but counting. Do the same for every character, not just t, and you have a table with one row per character. That table is the whole model.

Now do it on real text. Over the million characters of Shakespeare used in this course, a q is followed by a u in all 563 cases, so the table says u with probability 1. After a space, t comes 14% of the time (think the, to, that), then a at 8% and s at 7%.

Looking further back

One character is very little to go on. After n, almost anything can follow. After kin, it is g four times out of five. So let the table look at the last two characters, or the last three, instead of only the last one.

Definitions

The context is the text the model uses to make its prediction. For a counting model it is the last few characters.

The order of a counting model is how many characters of context it uses. Order 0 uses none and only knows how common each character is overall. Order 3 has one row for every three-character context it has seen.

A language model made of such tallies is called an n-gram model, or count table. The text it learns from (here, counts) is its training text.

A language model made of tallies

Try this. Press Write at order 0, then 1, then 3, then 5. The text goes from letter soup to something that looks like English. At each order, also type a context in the box and look at the bars: they are one row of the table. Then read the two numbers under the slider at order 5.

What you should have seen. At order 0 the output is letters in roughly the right proportions, with spaces in roughly the right places, and nothing else. At order 3, real short words appear. At order 5 whole phrases look like Shakespeare. That looks like progress, but the readout tells a different story: at order 5 most of the contexts in the table were seen only once or twice. When a context was seen once, there is exactly one continuation on record, with probability 1, and the model has no choice but to copy the book. It is not writing. It is quoting.

A ruler for language models

Looking at the output is not enough to tell a good model from a copying machine: the copying machine produces the best-looking text of all. We need a number. Here is the test that all of machine learning for language rests on.

Take some real text that the model has never seen. Walk through it one character at a time. At each position, ask the model for its distribution over the next character, and look up the probability it gave to the character that actually came next. A good model gives high probability to what really happens.

Definition

Text kept away from the model during learning, used only to test it, is called held-out text (also unseen, validation or test text). Testing on held-out text is the only fair test: a model can be perfect on text it has memorised and useless on anything new.

Each position gives us one probability, p. We need to turn it into a penalty: small when p is close to 1 (the model saw it coming), large when p is close to 0 (the model was caught out). The standard choice is:

surprise = −log p

If you have not used a logarithm in a while, open the refresher below. Otherwise, the experiment after it shows what this formula does.

Refresher: what a logarithm is

A logarithm answers the question "what power do I need?". Here we use the natural logarithm, written log, whose base is the number e ≈ 2.718. log x is the power you must raise e to in order to get x: log 1 = 0 because e0 = 1, and log e = 1.

Three facts are all this course needs:

  • log 1 = 0, and the log of a number between 0 and 1 is negative. That is why there is a minus sign in −log p: it makes the surprise positive.
  • As p goes towards 0, log p goes towards minus infinity, so the surprise grows without limit.
  • log(a × b) = log a + log b. Logarithms turn multiplication into addition.

How surprise depends on probability

Try this. Drag the slider: it is the probability the model gave to the character that really came next. Start at 1 and move down to 0.5, 0.1, 0.01.

What you should have seen. The curve is flat near p = 1 and shoots up near 0. Going from probability 1 to 0.5 costs 0.69. Going from 0.02 to 0.01 also costs 0.69: every time the probability is halved, the surprise goes up by the same amount. Being confidently wrong is punished hardest.

From one surprise to a score

A model's score on a text is its average surprise over all positions. Here is a worked example with a text of just three characters, with made-up probabilities:

Average2.23

One bad prediction dominates the average. That is deliberate: a model that is sometimes very wrong is a bad model.

Definition

The loss, also called cross-entropy, is the average surprise of a model over a text. Lower is better. Measured on held-out text, it is the single number used to compare, and later to train, every model in this course.

loss = average over positions of ( −log p(the character that actually came next) )

Why a logarithm, and not simply 1 − p? Because of the last fact in the refresher. The probability the model gives to the whole three-character text is the product 0.5 × 0.25 × 0.01 = 0.00125, and −log 0.00125 = 6.69 = 0.69 + 1.39 + 4.61. The surprise of a whole text is exactly the sum of the surprises of its characters. That is what you want from a ruler: two halves of a text add up to the whole.

The number to beat. A model that knows nothing gives each of our 65 characters the same probability, 1/65. Its surprise is −log(1/65) = log 65 ≈ 4.17 at every position, so its loss is 4.17. Any model worth having must score below that.

Where is the model surprised?

Try this. Each character is shaded by its surprise: darker means the model did not see it coming. Switch between text the model counted (its training text) and text it never saw (held-out text), at order 1 and then at order 5, and note the loss printed under the text each time.

What you should have seen. At order 1 the two texts look alike and the two losses are close: the model is equally mediocre everywhere. At order 5 the gap is plain. On text it counted, it is almost never surprised, because it has the answer on record. On new text from the same play it stumbles constantly, because many of the five-character contexts it meets were never in its table at all.

Definition

When a model does much better on its training text than on held-out text, it has learned the text instead of the patterns in it. This is called memorisation or overfitting. Doing well on new text is called generalisation. Generalisation is the only thing that matters, because new text is the only thing you will ever use a model on.

The wall

The widgets above count over a 60,000-character excerpt to stay light in your browser. Here is the same experiment on the full text, run by code/ch01_ngram.py: count on the first 90% of the text (about a million characters), and measure the loss on the rest.

Loss by order, on the full text

Blue: loss on the counted text. Pink: loss on held-out text. The dashed line is the know-nothing baseline, 4.17.

Read the two curves from left to right. The blue curve, on counted text, falls forever: with more context, the table remembers its training text more and more exactly. The pink curve, on held-out text, falls until order 4, reaches its best value of 1.68, and then climbs. Beyond order 4, more context makes the model worse at the only thing we care about. Why?

Too many rows, not enough text

Count how many rows the table could need. With 65 possible characters there are 65 contexts of one character. For two characters, each of the 65 first characters can be followed by any of 65 second ones: 65 × 65 = 4,225 contexts. Every extra character multiplies by 65 again.

Two things go wrong as the order grows, and you can measure both:

  • Contexts the table has never seen. At order 4, 2.8% of the contexts in the held-out text never appeared in the training text. At order 6 it is 18.5%, nearly one position in five. For those, the table has no row and can only shrug.
  • Contexts seen too few times. At order 6, 58% of the rows in the table were seen exactly once. A row seen once claims, with total confidence, that the one thing that happened is the only thing that can happen. On new text that confidence is usually wrong, and confident mistakes are what the loss punishes hardest.

Here is a real case. The held-out text contains the line Good morrow, neighbour Baptista. The training text contains neighbour many times and many words starting with B, but never the six characters bour B. An order-6 table looking at that context finds an empty row. It has nothing to say about what comes next, even though "a capital letter after neighbour starts a name, and names are often followed by a vowel" is exactly the kind of pattern it could have learned from elsewhere.

Definition

A table whose possible rows are mostly empty is called sparse. Sparsity is why the held-out loss climbs: with every extra character of context, the number of possible rows multiplies by 65, while the amount of text stays the same.

This is the wall, and its cause is that a table treats every context as unrelated to every other. It may have seen the king a hundred times and the duke a hundred times, and that tells it nothing about the earl, even though all three behave alike. To get past the wall we need a model that treats similar contexts similarly. Instead of looking the answer up in a table, it must compute it, with a formula that contains adjustable numbers, tuned on the data.

Looking ahead

Those adjustable numbers are called parameters (or weights). The model built in this course has 345,504 of them. Chapter 3 shows how to tune them, chapter 4 how similar characters end up with similar numbers, and chapters 5 and 6 build the formula itself.

Build it

Here is the whole model in Python. fit walks through the training text and, at each position, adds one to the tally of "this character followed this context". prob turns one row of tallies into a probability. loss is the average surprise from the ruler above.

def fit(order):
    """counts[context][next_char] = how often next_char followed context."""
    counts = defaultdict(Counter)
    for i in range(order, len(train)):
        counts[train[i - order:i]][train[i]] += 1
    return counts

def prob(counts, context, ch, alpha=0.01):
    """P(ch | context), with a tiny bit of probability reserved for the unseen."""
    c = counts.get(context, {})
    return (c.get(ch, 0) + alpha) / (sum(c.values()) + alpha * V)

def loss(counts, order, s):
    """Cross-entropy: the average surprise, -log p(what actually came next)."""
    return -sum(math.log(prob(counts, s[i - order:i], s[i])) for i in range(order, len(s))) / (len(s) - order)

From code/ch01_ngram.py. The whole model is a dictionary of counters.

One detail in prob: the small number alpha is added to every tally. Without it, a character never seen after a context would get probability exactly 0, and its surprise, −log 0, would be infinite: a single unexpected character would make the loss infinite. Adding 0.01 everywhere keeps every probability slightly above zero. The second function is the writing loop from the first figure: look up the row, roll the weighted dice, append.

def sample(counts, order, n=120, seed=0):
    rnd = random.Random(seed)
    out = train[:order]                        # seed with the first few characters
    for _ in range(n):
        c = counts.get(out[len(out) - order:], None)
        if not c:                              # never saw this context: the table has no answer
            out += rnd.choice(train)
            continue
        chars, weights = zip(*c.items())
        out += rnd.choices(chars, weights)[0]  # roll the weighted dice
    return out[order:]

Writing is the loop: look up, roll, append.

In short

  • A language model returns a probability distribution over the next symbol. Writing is a loop around it: sample, append, repeat.
  • The simplest model counts what followed each context in the training text.
  • Models are measured by their loss: the average surprise, −log p, on held-out text. Know-nothing guessing scores 4.17 here.
  • Longer contexts help until the table becomes sparse: possible contexts multiply by 65 per character, the text does not. The best count table reaches 1.68.
  • To do better, a model must treat similar contexts similarly: compute its answer with tuned parameters instead of looking it up.

What this chapter glossed over

The ruler measures imitation. A low loss means the model predicts text that resembles its training text. It says nothing about whether the text is true, kind or useful. Keep this in mind when the models get large: the ruler does not change.

Units. Losses here use the natural logarithm, so the unit is the nat. You will also meet bits (log base 2) and perplexity, which is eloss: the number of equally likely options the model is effectively hesitating between. A loss of 1.68 is a perplexity of 5.4 characters: at its best, the count table is as uncertain as if it were choosing among about five equally likely characters at every step.

Unseen is not impossible. A raw count of zero would mean probability zero and infinite surprise. The code adds a tiny constant to every count so that nothing is ever ruled out completely.

Check yourself

Why does uniform guessing cost exactly log 65?

Each of the 65 characters gets probability 1/65, so the surprise at every position is −log(1/65) = log 65 ≈ 4.17, whatever the text says.

The order-6 model scores 0.90 on counted text and 2.24 on unseen text. Which number describes the model?

The second. The first measures memory. Only text the model has not seen tells you how it will behave on anything new, which is the only situation you will ever use it in.

A real model reads 100,000 tokens of context. Could a count table do that?

No. Even with our tiny 65-symbol alphabet, the table for 6 characters of context already has 75 billion possible rows. The idea has to change, not scale.
