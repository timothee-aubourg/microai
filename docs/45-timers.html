Chapter 45

Waking itself up

"If nobody replies by tomorrow, remind me." Nothing will happen tomorrow to wake the agent. So what does?

Chapter 44 woke the agent when something arrived from outside. Much of what an assistant promises is the opposite: something should happen when nothing has arrived. Check again in two hours. Send the summary every morning. For that the agent sets its own alarms, and a small, plain program makes sure they ring.

Definitions

A follow-up is a deadline the agent sets itself, once: "if there is no reply by 5 pm, look again".

A schedule is a task that repeats: "every weekday at 9, send the summary".

A scheduler is a program that looks at the stored timers every few minutes. For each one that is due, it writes an event into the inbox of chapter 44, marks a follow-up as done, and moves a schedule on to its next time.

By hand: three timers and a scheduler that looks every 15 minutes

The scheduler looks at :00, :15, :30 and :45. At 10:07 the agent sets a follow-up for two hours later: due at 12:07. The scheduler next looks at 12:15, so it fires 8 minutes late. A schedule "every day at 9:00" is due exactly when the scheduler looks, so it fires on time. On average a timer fires half the time between two looks late, and at worst a whole one.

Now the scheduler is down from 6:00 to 11:00. The 9:00 summary was due while nobody looked. When it comes back at 11:00 it finds the schedule overdue. It can fire it now, once, and move on to tomorrow at 9:00. For a daily summary that is right. For an hourly one, five were missed: fire five summaries at 11:00, or one?

Firing every missed time sends six summaries at once: the five missed and the one due now, all saying nearly the same thing. For a summary, once is right. For something that must happen every time, such as recording each hour's reading, all of them is right. The schedule has to say which it is.

A morning of timers

Try this. Move the time between looks and watch how late the timers fire. Then switch the outage on and compare the two ways to handle missed times.

What you should have seen. Each timer fires at the first look after it is due, so the gaps grow with the time between looks. With the outage on, nothing fires while the scheduler is down. When it comes back, "all" fires every missed hourly time together, and "once" fires one.

At full size

  • Timers in their own table. Follow-ups and schedules sit in a store separate from the inbox, with their next due time, so that a quick query finds what is due.
  • Build it or use one. A loop that queries every minute is enough for one person's agent. Durable execution engines and job schedulers provide timers that survive restarts as a service.
  • The agent sets its own follow-ups with a tool, "remind me at", whose description says what a follow-up is for. A follow-up that fires and finds its reason gone (the reply arrived) should do nothing, quietly.
  • Time zones and calendars. "Every weekday at 9" means the person's 9, which moves when they travel and when clocks change. Real schedules are stored with a time zone.

What this chapter glossed over

It is a simulation with chosen numbers of timers, restarts and outages.

A restart loses timers only if they were in memory. Saving them to a file on every change also works for one worker, and fails in the same way as chapter 44's memory when there are several.

"Late" ignores the run itself. After the scheduler writes the event, the inbox and the worker of chapter 44 add their own wait.

In short

  • An agent wakes itself with timers: follow-ups that fire once and schedules that repeat.
  • A scheduler looks at the stored timers every few minutes and turns each due one into an inbox event. Timers fire half the time between looks late on average.
  • Timers kept in the worker's memory lost 119 of 227 follow-ups to two restarts a day. In a store of their own, none.
  • After an outage, firing every missed time sent six hourly summaries at once. Each schedule has to say whether a missed time is fired once or every time.

Check yourself

Why does the scheduler write an event into the inbox instead of starting a run itself?

So that a timer is handled like every other event: one worker, one run at a time, a record that survives a crash. A scheduler that started runs itself would bring back the overlapping runs of chapter 44.

A scheduler looks every 30 minutes. A follow-up is due at 14:31. When does it fire?

At 15:00, 29 minutes late, if the looks fall on the hour and the half hour. On average a timer is 15 minutes late with this scheduler.

The agent promised "I'll check again tomorrow", and the worker was redeployed overnight. What decides whether the promise is kept?

Where the follow-up was stored. In the worker's memory it vanished with the redeploy. In a store of its own it is still there, and the scheduler fires it.

Name a schedule where every missed time should be fired, and one where only one should.

Every time: recording a reading each hour, where each hour's value is needed. Once: a summary of what is new, where five summaries in a row would repeat each other.
