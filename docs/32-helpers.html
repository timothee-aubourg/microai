Chapter 32

Many hands

A job is too big for one context. Can it be split among several agents, and what does the split cost?

Chapter 16 showed the context filling up as an agent works, and what it costs to carry everything along. Our own agent makes the limit easy to see. Its window is 73 characters, and one sum with its tool call and its answer takes about 66 of them. Ask it to "add up: 23 times 45, 17 minus 3, 8 plus 9" and no arrangement of that work fits in one window.

The way out is the one people use. Nobody holds a whole project in their head. One person splits it, hands each piece to someone who works on that piece alone, and collects the results. For agents the pieces are called sub-agents, and the idea is in every current coding and research assistant.

Definitions

A sub-agent is an agent started by another agent to do one part of a job. It begins with an empty context, receives only its part, and returns only its result.

The lead is the agent that splits the job, starts the sub-agents and combines what they return. It is also called an orchestrator.

The window is 73 characters. With helpers, no context ever exceeds 70, however large the job. Without them, the working of every part would have to sit in one context: 230 characters for three parts, 456 for six. The lead's notes grow too, by about twenty characters a part, because a result is short. This is the first thing sub-agents buy: the working of a part is thrown away when the part is done, and only its result is kept.

By hand: chances multiply

The careless helper is right about half the time. For a job of three parts, all three must be right.

0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.125, or 25 jobs in 200

The measurement gives 22. The trained helper is right about 98.5% of the time, which sounds like enough. Over five parts it is 0.985 multiplied by itself five times, 0.93: about 185 jobs in 200, and the measurement gives 188. A helper that is nearly always right is not nearly always right five times running.

This is the second thing to know about splitting a job. Long jobs demand far more reliable steps than short ones, and every method of the last chapters that made one step more reliable matters more here than anywhere: training (chapter 24), and a check that asks again (chapter 25), which takes the careless helper from 22 to 181 on three parts.

Split a job

Try this. Run jobs of one part with the careless helper, then of three, then of six. Then switch to the trained helper and repeat.

What you should have seen. With the careless helper about half the one-part jobs are right, and almost none of the six-part jobs: each extra part halves the chance again. With the trained helper nearly every job is right at one part and a few fail at six. Look at the contexts printed under a job. Each helper's is short and separate, and the lead's line holds only numbers.

  • More tokens in total. Each helper reads its own question and writes its own answer, and a check that asks again roughly doubles that. Sub-agents save the lead's context. They do not save work.
  • Less waiting. The parts do not depend on each other, so the helpers can run at the same time, in one batch (chapter 27). The job then takes as long as its slowest part, not the sum of all of them.
  • A narrow hand-off. A helper knows only what the lead wrote down for it. Here that is the whole of its part. In real work the lead knows things it did not think to pass on, and the helper cannot ask.

At full size

In current assistants the lead is a model. It reads the request, writes a short brief for each sub-agent, and reads back their summaries. A sub-agent may read twenty files or run a long search to produce a paragraph. Those files never enter the lead's context, which is how one assistant can work through far more material than its window holds (chapter 16).

The same three facts hold. The working is thrown away and only the result is kept, so the brief and the summary have to carry everything that matters. The total cost in tokens goes up. And reliability multiplies, so systems of this kind lean on checks at every hand-off: tests that are run, results that are verified before they are combined.

When the split is fixed in advance, as here, the lead can be ordinary code, and the result is easier to predict and to test. A model as lead is for jobs whose split cannot be known beforehand.

Build it

def helper(run, part, rng, tries=1):
    """One helper: a fresh context, one part of the job. Returns its number (or None) and the text it used.
    With tries > 1 it is asked again until its answer rests on a tool result (the check of chapter 25)."""
    used = 0
    for _ in range(tries):
        e = post.episode(run, f'what is {part}?', rng)
        used += len(e['text'])
        if tries == 1 or e['call'] is not None:
            break
    last = e['answer'].rstrip('.').split(' ')[-1]
    return (int(last) if last.lstrip('-').isdigit() else None), used, len(e['text'])
def lead(run, request, rng, tries=1):
    """The lead: split the job, hand out the parts, add up what comes back. It never sees a helper's working."""
    parts = request[len('add up: '):].split(', ')
    results = [helper(run, part, rng, tries) for part in parts]        # each could run at the same time
    numbers = [r[0] for r in results]
    total = None if None in numbers else sum(numbers)                  # the lead's own tool call
    return {'total': total, 'tokens': sum(r[1] for r in results), 'largest': max(r[2] for r in results),
            'lead_context': len(request) + sum(len(str(n)) + 3 for n in numbers) + len(str(total)) + 12}

What this chapter glossed over

The lead is code. Splitting at commas needs no model. Our small model could not plan a split, and a lead that is a model makes mistakes of its own: a bad split, a missing brief.

The parts were independent. No helper needed another's result. When parts depend on each other they must run in order, and a mistake early on spoils everything after it.

Nothing was lost in the hand-off. A sum is a complete brief. The losses of real hand-offs, where the helper lacks context the lead had, are not measured here.

One run. 200 jobs for each size and each helper, one seed. Counts move by a few with another.

In short

  • A sub-agent does one part of a job in its own empty context and returns only its result. The lead splits the job and combines the results.
  • Contexts stay small: never more than 70 characters here, where one shared context would have needed 456 for six parts.
  • Reliability multiplies. A helper right half the time gets 22 of 200 three-part jobs right. A helper right 98.5% of the time gets 188 of 200 five-part jobs right.
  • The total number of tokens goes up, the waiting time can go down because helpers run together, and a helper knows only what the lead wrote for it.

Check yourself

The helpers did all the work with the calculator. Why does the lead's context not contain any tool calls for the parts?

Each helper worked in its own context and handed back one number. The calls and results that produced the number stayed in the helper's context, which is discarded when it finishes.

A helper is right 90% of the time. How often is a job of seven parts right?

0.9 multiplied by itself seven times is 0.48: less than half. Each part must be right for the job to be right, so the chances multiply.

Splitting a job among helpers used more tokens than doing it in one context would have. What was gained?

The job became possible at all, since no single context could hold it, and the parts can run at the same time. Sub-agents trade tokens for context and for time.

The lead tells a helper "fix the failing test" and nothing else. What can go wrong that could not go wrong in this chapter's jobs?

The helper lacks what the lead knew: which test, what was already tried, what must not be changed. Here each part was a complete question. A real brief has to carry that context in words, and what is left out is lost.
