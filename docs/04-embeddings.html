Chapter 4

Meaning as direction

To the computer a token is an ID number: a is 39, e is 43, z is 64. How can arithmetic on arbitrary ID numbers ever discover that a and e behave alike?

It cannot. The IDs are just positions in a list. If the formula multiplied them directly, it would be treating z as "more" than a, which means nothing.

So the very first step of the model throws the ID away and replaces it by a list of numbers. This chapter is about that list: what it is, how two such lists are compared, and what the trained model ended up putting in them.

Definitions

A vector is a list of numbers in a fixed order, such as (0.9, 0.1). The count of numbers in it is its number of dimensions. A vector of two numbers can be drawn as an arrow on paper: go 0.9 to the right and 0.1 up.

An embedding is the vector that stands for a token inside the model. The embedding table holds one row per token of the vocabulary, and each row is that token's embedding.

By hand: looking a token up

Take a vocabulary of three tokens and give each a vector of two numbers. The numbers here are made up.

Token IDTokenIts row of the table
0a(0.9, 0.1)
1e(0.8, 0.3)
2z(−0.5, 0.7)

The lookup is as simple as it sounds. The text ze is the IDs 2, 1, so the model takes row 2 and row 1: (−0.5, 0.7) and (0.8, 0.3). From here on it never uses the IDs again. Everything that follows is arithmetic on these vectors.

The model of this course does the same with larger numbers: 65 tokens, and 96 numbers per token instead of two. Its table therefore holds 65 × 96 = 6,240 numbers.

The important part is that these numbers are parameters, in the sense of chapter 3. They start as random noise, and gradient descent adjusts them like any other weight. Nobody tells the model that vowels are alike. But if giving a and e similar rows lowers the loss, their slopes push the two rows together.

The one operation that compares vectors

What does "similar rows" mean? Nearly everything the model does with vectors comes down to one operation.

Definitions

The dot product of two vectors is one number: multiply the two vectors entry by entry and add up the results.

The length of a vector is the length of its arrow: square every entry, add, and take the square root.

The cosine of two vectors is their dot product divided by both lengths. It is 1 when the arrows point the same way, 0 when they are at right angles, and −1 when they point in opposite directions, whatever their lengths.

a · b = a1b1 + a2b2 + …     cosine = a · b / ( |a| × |b| )

By hand: which rows are alike?

With the table above:

PairDot productCosine
a and e0.9 × 0.8 + 0.1 × 0.3 = 0.750.75 / (0.91 × 0.85) = 0.97
a and z0.9 × −0.5 + 0.1 × 0.7 = −0.38−0.38 / (0.91 × 0.86) = −0.49

The lengths come from the definition: for a, the square root of 0.9² + 0.1² is 0.91. The rows of a and e point almost the same way, with a cosine of 0.97. The row of z points away from both. "Similar" now has a number.

The dot product, by hand

Try this. Read the numbers under the arrows as they stand. Then drag the arrow tips. Make the arrows perpendicular. Point them in opposite directions. Last, stretch one arrow without turning it.

What you should have seen. The arrows start at (2.4, 0.8) and (1.0, 2.0): the dot product is 2.4 × 1.0 + 0.8 × 2.0 = 4.00, the lengths are 2.53 and 2.24, and the cosine is 0.71, an angle of 45°. Perpendicular arrows give a dot product of 0. Opposite arrows give a negative one. Stretching an arrow makes the dot product grow although the angle has not changed, and the cosine stays where it was. The cosine ignores length. The dot product does not.

Keep both readings in mind. The raw dot product is what the model computes, because it is cheap. The cosine is what you use when you only care about direction. Chapter 5 uses dot products to decide which tokens matter to each other, and chapter 11 uses cosines to find documents.

What our model learned

Here is the embedding table of the trained model, the one whose training chapter 7 follows: 65 characters, each a point in 96 dimensions. A screen has two. So the picture below is a flattened view: it keeps the two directions along which the points spread the most, and drops the other 94. Tapping a character lists its nearest neighbours, measured by cosine in the full 96 dimensions.

The 65 characters, as the model sees them

Try this. Tap e, then t, then a full stop, and read who their neighbours are. Then tap any character you like.

What you should have seen. The nearest neighbours of e are a (cosine 0.33), i (0.27) and o (0.25). The nearest neighbour of t is T (0.32), and that of h is H. The nearest neighbour of the full stop is the question mark, at 0.75, followed by the exclamation mark and the semicolon.

Three things stand out, and none of them was programmed. The vowels a, e, i, o are each other's nearest neighbours. The punctuation marks form the tightest group of all: to this model a full stop and a question mark are nearly the same thing, which is fair, since the same kind of text follows both. And many lowercase letters have their own capital as nearest neighbour. Nobody told the model that T is a capital t. It found out because the two are followed by similar text.

This is what chapter 1 asked for at its wall: a model that treats similar contexts similarly. A count table had one unrelated row for every context. Here, tokens that behave alike have vectors that point alike, so what the model learns about one carries over to the other.

Two more jobs for the same idea

Position

The table gives a the same vector wherever it stands. That loses something a count table had for free. Look at two short texts, ze and ez, with the small table above:

TextVectors the model receives
ze(−0.5, 0.7) and (0.8, 0.3)
ez(0.8, 0.3) and (−0.5, 0.7)

The same two vectors, in another order. You might think the order of the list is enough. It is not, because of how the next chapter works: there, each token collects information from the others by comparing vectors, and a comparison of two vectors gives the same number wherever the two tokens stand. To that step, a text is a bag of vectors. dog bites man and man bites dog would be the same bag.

So the place of each token has to be written into its vector before anything else happens.

Definition

A position embedding is a vector that stands for a place in the text: first, second, third. It is added to the token's embedding.

Our model has a second table for this, with one row per position. It reads at most 64 characters at once, so the table has 64 rows of 96 numbers: 6,144 more parameters, random at the start and learned like the first table.

By hand: adding the position

Adding two vectors means adding them entry by entry. Suppose the position table of our small example is this:

PositionIts row
first(0.1, 0.0)
second(0.0, 0.2)

Then the two texts no longer give the same vectors:

TextFirst token + first positionSecond token + second position
ze(−0.5, 0.7) + (0.1, 0.0) = (−0.4, 0.7)(0.8, 0.3) + (0.0, 0.2) = (0.8, 0.5)
ez(0.8, 0.3) + (0.1, 0.0) = (0.9, 0.3)(−0.5, 0.7) + (0.0, 0.2) = (−0.5, 0.9)

An e in first place is now (0.9, 0.3) and an e in second place is (0.8, 0.5): close to each other, since it is the same letter, and not identical, since it stands somewhere else. Each sum is the vector that the rest of the model works on. It carries both facts at once, which token and where.

Nothing tells the model what a position is. The 64 rows start as noise, with no hint that position 9 comes after position 8. The next experiment looks at what training made of them.

What the position table learned

Try this. The bars compare the row of one position with the rows of positions further along. Start at position 10 and read the bars from top to bottom. Then move the slider to 1, and to 30.

What you should have seen. At position 10, the row is nearly the same as its neighbour's, with a cosine of 0.99, and still very close 8 places away. Further along the likeness fades, and 32 places away the cosine is negative: the two rows point in different directions. Averaged over the whole table, neighbouring positions have a cosine of 0.96, positions 8 apart 0.82, 16 apart 0.47 and 32 apart −0.37. The model has laid its positions out in order, like marks along a line, although nobody told it they were ordered. Position 1 is the exception: its row resembles no other very much (0.71 at best), because the first character of a text is a special place with nothing before it.

The order was learned for the same reason the vowels came together. Characters 9 and 10 of a passage are in almost the same situation, so rows that are almost the same serve them both.

Output

Now jump to the far end of the model. Chapters 5 and 6 fill in the middle. All that matters here is what comes out of it: for the position being predicted, one final vector of 96 numbers, which is the model's summary of the text so far.

A vector is not a prediction. Chapter 1 asked for a probability for every possible next token, so the final vector must become 65 numbers, one per token. The model gets them with the dot product again: the score of a token is the dot product of the final vector with that token's row of the embedding table. A high score means the final vector points along that token's row.

By hand, with the small table: suppose the final vector is (1.0, 0.2).

TokenIts rowScore: dot product with (1.0, 0.2)
a(0.9, 0.1)0.9 + 0.02 = 0.92
e(0.8, 0.3)0.8 + 0.06 = 0.86
z(−0.5, 0.7)−0.5 + 0.14 = −0.36

The highest score goes to a, the token whose row points most along the final vector. The prediction is, quite literally, the answer to "which token's direction does my output point along?" Chapter 5 shows how scores become the probabilities of chapter 1.

Why the same table? It could have been a second one, and some models do that. Using one table has two effects. It saves 6,240 parameters. And it ties the two meanings of a row together: tokens that are read alike, because their rows are close, are also predicted alike, because the final vector that scores high with one scores high with the other. In the small table, e got 0.86, right behind a.

Definition

Weight tying is using the same table at both ends of the model: to turn tokens into vectors at the input, and to turn the final vector into scores at the output.

Build it

p = {}
p['wte'] = w(vocab_size, d)               # token embedding  (also the output layer)
p['wpe'] = w(block_size, d)               # position embedding
for i in range(n_layer):
    p[f'h{i}.ln1.g'] = Tensor(np.ones(d));  p[f'h{i}.ln1.b'] = Tensor(np.zeros(d))
    p[f'h{i}.attn.wq'] = w(d, d)          # what am I looking for?
    p[f'h{i}.attn.wk'] = w(d, d)          # what do I contain?
    p[f'h{i}.attn.wv'] = w(d, d)          # what do I hand over if chosen?
    p[f'h{i}.attn.wo'] = w(d, d, std=res_std)
    p[f'h{i}.ln2.g'] = Tensor(np.ones(d));  p[f'h{i}.ln2.b'] = Tensor(np.zeros(d))
    p[f'h{i}.mlp.w1'] = w(d, 4 * d)       # expand...
    p[f'h{i}.mlp.w2'] = w(4 * d, d, std=res_std)   # ...and project back
p['ln_f.g'] = Tensor(np.ones(d));  p['ln_f.b'] = Tensor(np.zeros(d))
self.p = p

From code/gpt.py. These few lines declare every parameter of the model. wte is the token embedding table and wpe the position table; in the code, d is the 96 and block_size the 64. Each is created as a tensor (chapter 3) filled with small random numbers. The other lines belong to chapters 5 and 6.

What this chapter glossed over

A projection is a shadow. The map keeps a fraction of the spread of the points and discards the rest. Two characters that sit together on the map can be far apart in the dimensions you cannot see. Trust the neighbour list over the picture.

This is only the starting point of meaning. The table gives each token one fixed vector, whatever its context. The p in spear and the p in hope enter the network identical. Making them different, according to their neighbours, is the job of chapters 5 and 6.

Modern models encode position differently. Instead of adding a learned row, most current models turn the vectors by an angle that depends on position. Chapter 30 builds that and compares it with the table used here. The purpose is the same.

In short

  • A token ID is replaced at once by a vector, its embedding: one row of a table of parameters. Ours has 65 rows of 96 numbers.
  • Two vectors are compared with the dot product: multiply entry by entry and add. The cosine is the same thing with the lengths divided out.
  • Training moves tokens that behave alike towards the same direction. In our model the nearest neighbour of e is a, of t is T, and of the full stop is the question mark.
  • A second table adds a position embedding, so the model knows where each token stands. Training put its 64 rows in order: neighbouring positions have a cosine of 0.96, positions 32 apart −0.37.
  • The output scores are dot products of the final vector with the rows of the same table, which is weight tying.

Check yourself

With the small table of this chapter, what is the dot product of the rows of e and z?

0.8 × −0.5 + 0.3 × 0.7 = −0.40 + 0.21 = −0.19. It is negative: the two rows point away from each other.

How many parameters are in our token table? And in the table of a model with 128,000 tokens and 4,096 numbers per token?

65 × 96 = 6,240. The large one has 128,000 × 4,096 ≈ 524 million: about 1,500 times our entire model, just for the first lookup.

Two vectors have dot product 0. What does the model "think" of them?

That they have nothing to do with each other: their arrows are at right angles, and the cosine is 0 too. In 96 dimensions there is a lot of room for vectors to be at right angles to one another.

Why is the output layer a set of dot products with the embedding rows?

It asks which token the final vector resembles. If the model turns the context into a vector pointing along the row of u, then u gets the highest score. One table then serves two purposes: turning tokens into vectors, and vectors back into tokens.
