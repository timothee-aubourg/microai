Chapter 43

Asking first

An agent is about to run a command. When should it stop and ask you first?

Coding agents stop and ask before they run some commands. Ask too rarely and a wrong command runs. Ask too often and people approve without reading. This chapter measures that trade on our own agent.

There are two separate reasons to ask. The first is the action itself: deleting a file or sending money cannot be undone, whoever wrote the request. That is a rule you write in the harness, and chapter 17 built one, a gate that checks each command against a list. The second reason is the model: it may have misread the request. Only the model can tell us how unsure it was, through the probabilities it gave its own tokens.

Definitions

Human in the loop means that a person approves some of the agent's actions before they run. The harness pauses, shows the action, and waits for a yes or a no.

The confidence of a call is the probability the model gave to it: the probabilities of each of its characters, multiplied together.

A confidence is calibrated when it matches how often the model is right. Of the calls it is 90% sure of, about 90 in 100 are right.

It gave sub a probability of 1.00 and hesitated over the digits.

The third row is the dangerous one. less means minus, the model wrote add, and it was sure. Only a threshold that makes the agent ask about nearly every call would stop it.

600 requests, half with slips

code/ch43_ask.py gives the agent 600 requests with number pairs it never saw in training. Half are worded exactly as in training. The other half carry the slips people make: tims or multiplied by for times, less or take away for minus, and for plus, or a word left out. For each request it records the call the model wants to make and the confidence of that call.

def call_and_confidence(run, question):
    """The call the model would make, and the probability it gave to that call, character by character.
    Returns (None, p) if it would answer without a tool; p is then its probability for not calling."""
    cache = run.new_cache()
    logits = one.feed(run, cache, '\nU ' + question + '\n')
    p, line, state, fmt = 1.0, '', START, [['C ', 'A ']] + one.CALL
    while True:
        legal = moves(fmt, state)
        if not legal:
            break
        ids = [run.stoi[c] for c in legal]
        z = logits[ids] - logits[ids].max()
        probs = np.exp(z) / np.exp(z).sum()            # the model's choice among the characters allowed here
        k = int(np.argmax(probs))
        p *= float(probs[k])                            # how sure it was of this character
        c = run.chars[ids[k]]
        state, line = legal[c], line + c
        logits = run.step(run.stoi[c], cache)
        if line == 'A ':
            return None, p
    return line[2:].strip(), p

Four rules for when to ask

def plain(question):
    """The harness's own check of the wording: exactly 'what is <n> <plus|minus|times> <n>?'."""
    w = question.split()
    return (len(w) == 5 and w[:2] == ['what', 'is'] and w[2].isdigit() and w[3] in one.WORDS
            and w[4].endswith('?') and w[4][:-1].isdigit())

def rule(name, threshold=0.0):
    """Each rule says, for one request, whether to ask the person before running the call."""
    return {'never': lambda r: False,
            'always': lambda r: True,
            'unsure': lambda r: r['p'] < threshold,
            'worded oddly': lambda r: not plain(r['q'])}[name]

A person who is asked is assumed to stop every wrong call. So a rule has two numbers: how often it asks, and how many wrong calls it lets through without asking.

  • Always asking is safe and useless. 557 questions to the person, 455 of them about calls that were right. People who approve that often stop reading, which brings the safety back to zero. This is called approval fatigue.
  • A confidence threshold buys a lot cheaply. Below 0.99, the agent asks about one call in four (146) and stops 89 of the 102 wrong calls.
  • The 13 that remain are expensive to stop. Every one of them is less read as plus or and read as minus, with a confidence above 0.99. A model is not unsure about what it misunderstands. It is unsure where its training left it between two answers, and less was not one of those places. Raising the threshold to 0.998 does catch all 13, but then the agent asks about 504 of the 557 calls.
  • The wording rule stops everything here, and only here. It works because the harness knows the one form the model was trained on. Real requests have no such form. At full size the equivalent is a separate check, such as a second model that judges the request (chapter 28).

At full size

  • Harnesses ask by kind of action first. Coding agents typically let reading run freely, ask before editing files or running commands, and let you allow specific commands for good. These are rules about what the action is, set by you: the first box of the figure.
  • The model's confidence is harder to reach. Some model interfaces return the probability of each token, and a harness can multiply them as we did. Many do not. A model can also be asked to state how sure it is in words, which is much less reliable than its token probabilities.
  • Overconfidence is the rule, not the exception. Large models trained to give helpful answers (chapter 24) tend to be more sure than they are right, as ours was between 0.9 and 0.99.
  • What to show the person matters. An approval shows the exact action, with its arguments, and why it was flagged. "Run this command?" with no context trains people to click yes.
  • Risk is probability times cost. A wrong sum costs little and a wrong deletion costs a lot. A sensible harness asks at a low confidence for cheap actions and at a high one, or always, for expensive ones.

What this chapter glossed over

The person is perfect here. Every wrong call that is asked about is stopped. Real people miss some, and miss more when they are asked often.

The confidence is of the call alone. It is the probability of the characters of the call, given that the model writes one. A fuller measure would include its probability of calling at all.

The slips are ours. A list of misspellings and synonyms written for this test, not a sample of how people really write.

One run, no sampling. 600 requests, the most likely character chosen at each step.

In short

  • Ask before an action for two reasons: the action cannot be undone (a rule you write), or the model may have misread the request (a number from the model).
  • The confidence of a call, the product of its character probabilities, separates right from wrong calls well: 8% right below 0.5, 97% above 0.99.
  • Asking below 0.99 asked about one call in four and stopped 89 of 102 wrong calls. Always asking stopped all of them at the price of 557 approvals.
  • Confidence barely helps against a confident misunderstanding: less read as plus at 0.996. Catching those took asking about 504 of 557 calls. They need a check on the request, not on the model's doubt.

Check yourself

A call is written with character probabilities 1.0, 0.9, 0.8 and 1.0. What is its confidence?

0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72. The characters the model was sure of do not lower it. The two uncertain ones do.

Why is "always ask" not the safest rule in practice?

Because a person asked about every action, nearly all of them right, stops reading. Here 455 of the 557 approvals would have been for right calls. Safety depends on the person paying attention when it matters.

The agent read "42 less 26" as plus with a confidence of 0.996. Why did the model not show any doubt?

It never saw "less" in training, so nothing pulled it towards minus. Its probabilities show doubt between answers it learned, not about meanings it never learned. Confidence measures the first, not the second.

An action deletes a database. What threshold should the agent use before running it?

None: ask every time, whatever the model's confidence. An action that cannot be undone is decided by the first box of the figure, a rule about the action, before the model's confidence is even looked at.
